# Cybersecurity vs Information Security

Cybersecurity and information security are closely related, and people often use the terms interchangeably. Both aim to reduce the chance that important information will be exposed, changed, lost, or made unavailable. The difference is mainly one of scope: **information security protects information in any form, while cybersecurity focuses on digital systems, networks, and data**.

Understanding that distinction helps businesses choose suitable controls rather than treating every risk as a software problem. A phishing attack may call for email protections and staff training; an unlocked filing cabinet may call for physical access controls. Many real-world risks require both.

## What is information security?

**Information security**, often shortened to InfoSec, is the practice of protecting information and the systems and processes that support it. Information can be digital, printed, spoken, or stored in another format. Its protection should account for the information’s full lifecycle: creation, access, use, storage, sharing, retention, and disposal.

A common way to describe information security goals is the **CIA triad**:

- **Confidentiality:** Only authorized people or systems can access the information.
- **Integrity:** Information remains accurate and is changed only in authorized ways.
- **Availability:** Authorized users can access information when they need it.

For example, a company may protect customer records by limiting access, keeping reliable backups, and checking that changes to account details are authorized. These controls support confidentiality, availability, and integrity together.

Information security is broader than technology. It can include policies, staff training, document handling, supplier requirements, physical safeguards, and technical controls. The exact responsibilities and terminology vary between organizations.

## What is cybersecurity?

**Cybersecurity** is the practice of protecting digital devices, applications, networks, accounts, and data from unauthorized access, disruption, misuse, or damage. It addresses threats that involve computers and connected technologies, such as phishing, malware, account compromise, software vulnerabilities, and denial-of-service attacks.

Cybersecurity measures can include:

- Multi-factor authentication and strong account-access controls.
- Secure configuration and timely software updates.
- Endpoint protection and network monitoring.
- Encryption for data in transit and, where appropriate, at rest.
- Backups and tested recovery procedures.
- Secure software development and vulnerability management.

Cybersecurity is not limited to attacks by outsiders. Accidental actions, compromised supplier accounts, misconfigured cloud services, and misuse by someone with legitimate access can also create cyber risks.

## Cybersecurity vs information security: the key differences

| Area | Information security | Cybersecurity |
|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Protecting information and its supporting processes | Protecting digital systems, networks, accounts, and data |
| Information formats | Digital, paper, verbal, and other forms | Primarily digital information and connected technology |
| Example risks | A lost paper file, unauthorized disclosure, or incorrect record | Ransomware, stolen credentials, or an exposed cloud service |
| Possible controls | Document procedures, access rules, staff training, physical security, and technical safeguards | Authentication, patching, monitoring, encryption, and incident response |
| Relationship | Broad discipline for managing information protection | Closely related discipline focused on digital threats and assets |

These are useful working definitions, not strict boundaries recognized identically everywhere. Some organizations place cybersecurity within a broader information security program; others use the terms in overlapping ways. The important thing is to define responsibilities and risks clearly.

## How the two disciplines overlap

Information security and cybersecurity share the same broad aim: protect valuable information and support the organization’s ability to operate. They also rely on many of the same principles, including access control, risk assessment, incident planning, and staff awareness.

Consider a customer database. Cybersecurity controls might include multi-factor authentication, secure configuration, logging, and tested backups. Information security also considers who is allowed to use the records, how long they should be retained, whether staff may export them, and how the information should be handled if it is printed or shared with a supplier.

A useful way to think about the relationship is that cybersecurity addresses many of the **digital ways information can be threatened**, while information security considers **the information and its protection across formats and processes**.

## Practical risks: two examples

### Example 1: A phishing email leads to account compromise

An employee receives a convincing message and enters their password on a fake sign-in page. An attacker uses the stolen credentials to access business files.

This is a cybersecurity incident because digital accounts and systems were targeted. It is also an information security issue because sensitive information may be exposed or altered. A layered response could include phishing-resistant authentication where feasible, email filtering, staff reporting procedures, limited account privileges, activity monitoring, and a plan to revoke access and investigate suspected exposure.

### Example 2: A printed report is left in a shared space

A printed report containing customer details is left unattended in a meeting room. No computer system was attacked, but unauthorized people may see the information.

This is an information security risk. Practical controls include limiting what gets printed, collecting documents promptly, using secure disposal, and setting clear rules for handling sensitive records. If the report was generated from a digital system, access controls and export monitoring may also help prevent or investigate the incident.

## Design choices that support both

Good security design starts with the information and the risks, rather than with a particular tool. These choices can help organizations make controls more effective:

1. **Identify important information and systems.** Know what data the organization holds, where it is stored, who uses it, and which services depend on it. Include paper records and third-party services where relevant.
2. **Assess likely threats and impact.** Consider accidental loss, unauthorized access, service disruption, human error, supplier issues, and deliberate attacks. Prioritize according to potential impact and realistic exposure.
3. **Apply least privilege.** Give people and systems only the access they need, and review access when roles change. This reduces the damage that a compromised or misused account can cause.
4. **Protect information throughout its lifecycle.** Set appropriate rules for collection, sharing, storage, retention, and secure disposal. Use encryption and other technical safeguards where they address a specific risk.
5. **Plan for disruption and recovery.** Keep backups appropriate to business needs, protect them from unauthorized changes, and test restoration. A backup that has never been tested may not support recovery as expected.
6. **Make responsibilities clear.** Define who handles security decisions, incident reporting, supplier reviews, and recovery tasks. A written policy is useful only when staff understand how to follow it.

## Security best practices for beginners

Whether you are an individual or helping a small organization, the following security best practices offer a practical starting point:

- Use unique passwords and a reputable password manager. Turn on multi-factor authentication, especially for email, financial, administrative, and cloud accounts.
- Install supported software updates promptly, including updates for phones, computers, browsers, and network equipment.
- Be cautious with unexpected links, attachments, payment requests, and requests for credentials. Verify unusual requests through a separate trusted channel.
- Back up important information and periodically confirm that you can restore it.
- Limit access to sensitive information. Remove accounts and permissions that are no longer needed.
- Lock devices and store physical records securely. Dispose of sensitive documents and storage media appropriately.
- Create a simple incident-reporting process. Make it easy for people to report a suspicious message, lost device, or accidental disclosure quickly.

No single control prevents every incident. Combining suitable technical, physical, and procedural measures is generally more effective than relying on one tool or one person’s vigilance.

## Which one should your organization focus on?

In most cases, this is not an either-or choice. An organization that handles information should consider information security across its operations and use cybersecurity controls to address risks to digital assets. The balance depends on what information the organization holds, how it operates, which systems it relies on, and the likely consequences of an incident.

For a small business, a practical first step is to list key information and services, identify who needs access, and note how each could be lost, exposed, altered, or made unavailable. Use that review to prioritize a manageable set of protections, then revisit it when systems, suppliers, or business needs change.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cybersecurity part of information security?

Often, yes. Many organizations treat cybersecurity as one part of a wider information security program because digital threats can put information at risk. Organizational structures and terminology differ, so clarify how responsibilities are assigned in your workplace.

### Can information security apply without computers?

Yes. Information security also applies to printed records, spoken conversations, and other non-digital information. Physical access rules, secure document storage, and careful disposal can all support information protection.

### Which is more important: cybersecurity or information security?

Neither is universally more important. The priority should be based on the information and services at risk. A business that depends heavily on online systems may need substantial cyber defenses, while one that handles sensitive physical records also needs appropriate handling and access procedures.

### Are cybersecurity and information security the same thing?

They overlap, and some people use the terms interchangeably. A useful distinction is that information security covers information in any format, while cybersecurity focuses on digital environments and threats. Definitions can vary by organization.

## Conclusion

The difference between cybersecurity and information security is primarily scope. Cybersecurity protects digital systems and data from cyber risks; information security protects information in digital and non-digital forms across its lifecycle. Both involve people, processes, and technology.

For practical security, identify what information matters, understand how it could be compromised or become unavailable, and choose protections that fit those risks. Combining sensible technical safeguards with clear procedures and physical protections helps create a more complete approach.
